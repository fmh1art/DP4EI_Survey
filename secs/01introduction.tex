\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Robot learning is entering a data-rich but not yet data-coherent regime.
Open X-Embodiment brings heterogeneous datasets into a common interface, DROID distributes real-world collection across institutions, and the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) reduces the need for continuous access to a target robot.
Recent generalist policies consequently train on data from different robots, tasks, environments, and forms of supervision~\cite{collaboration2023open_2310_08864,khazatsky2024droid_2403_12945,chi2024universal_2402_10329,kim2024openvla_2406_09246,team2024octo_2405_12213}.
This expansion is often summarized by episode counts or hours, but neither quantity explains what a model can learn from the data.
A large collection can still mix incompatible coordinate frames, control modes, frequencies, language descriptions, success criteria, operator styles, and physical assumptions.
Even a technically valid record may duplicate behavior already covered, use actions that do not transfer to the target robot, or provide supervision that is suitable only for one training stage.

We use \emph{raw embodied record} for the observations, states, actions, language, outcome, and metadata stored for an episode or a segment of behavior.
Before such a record is used for learning, a pipeline may repair timestamps, align coordinates, locate task boundaries, add labels, remove defects, set a sampling weight, assign a training role, or generate a related example.
This survey uses \emph{data preparation for embodied intelligence} for these decisions.
The definition includes decisions made before training and later decisions triggered by model evaluation or deployment.
It does not treat every part of robot learning as data preparation.

Preparation changes more than the contents of a saved dataset.
It also determines which examples a learner sees, how often they are sampled, and whether they are used for broad pretraining, robot alignment, task adaptation, recovery, safety, or evaluation.
We call this combination of prepared examples, sampling weights, and training roles the \emph{training distribution}.
The term does not imply that the distribution has a simple analytic form.
It describes the data that are actually presented to the optimizer and the rules used to present them.
When evaluation of a trained or deployed model changes a later collection or preparation decision, we call the resulting closed loop a \emph{data engine}.
When several rounds of this loop repeatedly revise the data used for training, we refer to the process as \emph{data self-evolution}.

This boundary is deliberately narrower than ``data for embodied AI.''
Our core scope is manipulation and generalist vision--language--action (VLA) learning.
Collection is included when its design changes compatibility, coverage, or the feedback available for later preparation.
Human video and simulation are included when they are converted into supervision for embodied policy learning.
Model and system reports are included when they disclose a data operation or a reproducible part of a data recipe.
We do not systematically review robot hardware, policy architectures, generic simulators, navigation, autonomous driving, or benchmarks that evaluate only a final model.
These topics appear only when they expose a preparation mechanism that transfers beyond one system.

\subsection{Why a Separate Survey Is Needed}

Adjacent reviews provide valuable maps, but they organize the field around different questions (Table~\ref{tab:adjacent-surveys}).
The Data Pyramid organizes sources, a recent VLA survey catalogs datasets and infrastructure, and a human-video survey organizes transfer mechanisms~\cite{ye2026data_2607_24744,wang2026vision_2604_23001,ma2026robot_2604_27621}.
Our focus is the decision made after a source is available: what should be changed, what evidence should guide the change, and how can we establish that the resulting data improve learning?
This decision has become a distinct research problem for three reasons.

First, data operations increasingly contain learned components.
Recent methods learn source-mixture weights, estimate redundancy or information, predict task progress, and approximate how a training example changes a target learner~\cite{hejna2024re_2408_14037,hejna2025robot_2502_08623,dass2025datamil_2505_09603,zhang2025scizor_2505_22626,xu2026athena_2606_16208}.
Their behavior depends on the model and calibration data used to make the decision, so the operation itself requires evaluation.

Second, foundation models can label and generate data at a scale that makes exhaustive human inspection impossible~\cite{blank2024scaling_2410_17772,mandlekar2023mimicgen_2310_17596,li2026xiaomi_2607_11643}.
The bottleneck then moves from producing examples to detecting semantic, geometric, and physical errors and deciding which generated examples can supervise robot actions.

Third, deployment is becoming a source of targeted training data.
Self-improving systems use successes, failures, and human interventions from the current policy to decide what to collect or train on next~\cite{bousmalis2023robocat_2306_11706,ahn2024autort_2401_12963,intelligence20250_2511_14759,pan2026sop_2601_03044,wang2026learning_2605_00416}.
Preparation is no longer always a one-time script because the model can change the data used to train its successor.

Industrial reports show the same change at system scale.
Qwen emphasizes representation, motion, and behavior alignment.
The $\pi$ family assigns different sources and quality levels to different training roles.
GR00T combines real demonstrations with generated and simulated expansion.
Gemini Robotics reports Motion Transfer, which aligns motion data across robot embodiments~\cite{yuan2026qwen_2606_17846,intelligence20260_2604_15483,nvidia2025gr00t_2503_14734,team2025gemini_2510_03342}.
These reports do not provide a common experimental setting and therefore do not support a numerical leaderboard.
They do show that data preparation has become a recurring design problem rather than an undocumented preprocessing detail.

\subsection{Central Thesis}

The central claim of this survey is that data quality is not a fixed property of a record.
A record is useful only in relation to a target robot, learner, task, training stage, and surrounding dataset.
We call this dependence \emph{conditional data utility}.
Quality is also multidimensional because technical validity, semantic correctness, action compatibility, physical feasibility, coverage, diversity, and safety describe different properties and can disagree.

A failed trajectory provides a concrete example.
It is harmful when every failed action is presented as an expert target.
The same trajectory can be useful when a valid prefix is used for skill learning, a retry is used for recovery training, or the failed outcome is used to train a reward or safety model.
Conversely, a smooth successful trajectory may add little when it repeats states already covered or uses an action convention that the target robot cannot execute.
Formal analyses show that state diversity, action divergence, and uncertainty in state transitions interact rather than produce one universal ordering of demonstrations~\cite{belkhale2023data_2306_02437}.
Empirical curation studies reach the same practical conclusion because removing a large fraction of a dataset helps in some settings but removes necessary coverage in others~\cite{hejna2025robot_2502_08623,bedi2026what_2606_10229}.

We organize this decision problem with four questions.
The \emph{operation axis} asks what is changed in the record or in its use during training.
The \emph{decision-signal axis} asks what evidence guides that operation, such as a rule, a human label, a pretrained model, or feedback from the target policy.
The \emph{utility-evidence axis} asks what the reported experiment establishes, from a change in a data property to an improvement after policy training or deployment.
The \emph{lifecycle axis} asks when the decision is made and whether people or an automated controller choose the next operation.
The technical review follows the operation axis, while the other three axes provide common dimensions for comparing methods at every stage.
Source type, modality, transformed object, granularity, and objective are recorded as additional attributes rather than forced into mutually exclusive categories.

\subsection{Contributions and Reader's Guide}

The survey makes four contributions.
First, it defines data preparation as the set of decisions that convert raw embodied records into training examples, sampling weights, and stage-specific roles.
This boundary separates data preparation from general data collection and policy architecture design.
Second, it provides an evidence map of 211 works with search records, reading-depth labels, and structured coding.
The resulting taxonomy compares operations by their decision signal, granularity, cost, validation, generalization, and failure modes rather than by headline performance.
Third, the survey reconstructs public industrial recipes and compares data engines by the feedback they observe, the data decision they make, and the controls used during model updates.
This comparison separates disclosed facts from within-paper evidence and from our cross-paper interpretation.
Fourth, the survey relates embodied preparation to language-model data engineering and develops a research agenda for evaluating data value, stabilizing closed-loop updates, and limiting the authority of autonomous preparation systems.
