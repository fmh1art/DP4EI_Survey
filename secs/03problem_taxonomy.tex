\section{Problem Formulation and Taxonomy}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{From Raw Records to Training Units}

The distinction introduced in Section~\ref{sec:introduction} can be stated without assuming that every source contains the same fields.
Let $T$ denote the length of an episode.
For a raw episode $e$, let $m$ contain its provenance, embodiment, sensor, calibration, operator, and timing metadata.
Let $o_{1:T}$, $s_{1:T}$, and $a_{1:T}$ denote observations, robot states, and actions.
The variables $\ell$ and $y$ denote language or task context and recorded outcomes or interventions.
We write:
\begin{equation}
e=(m,\,o_{1:T},\,s_{1:T},\,a_{1:T},\,\ell,\,y).
\end{equation}
Some fields may be absent or unreliable.
Human video often lacks robot state and action, while generated data can fill every field but still violate geometry or dynamics.

Let $\rawdata$ denote a collection of raw records and $\traindata$ the units made available to a learner.
Let $c$ denote the training context: the target learner, robot embodiment, evaluation tasks, and training stage.
A preparation pipeline $\pipeline_c$ chosen for this context applies one or more operators to obtain:
\begin{equation}
\traindata=\pipeline_c(\rawdata).
\label{eq:prep-map}
\end{equation}
These factors matter because the same record may be useful for visual pretraining but invalid as an action target for a particular robot.

Equation~\ref{eq:prep-map} does not require one input episode to remain one output example.
An operator can keep an episode intact, split it into behavior segments, extract individual transitions, pair it with another modality, or generate several alternatives.
We use \emph{training unit} for whichever of these objects is actually presented to the learner.
A unit may also carry a sampling weight and a training role.
The role determines whether it supports broad pretraining, robot alignment, task learning, recovery, or evaluation.
Sending the same unit to a different stage or objective is called \emph{routing}.
Provenance and version metadata are stored with the prepared dataset so that each unit can be traced back to its raw source and transformations.
We call this trace the unit's \emph{lineage}.

The primary objective is to improve the trained policy, not to maximize the fraction of records that pass a filter.
For a fixed learner $f$ and training budget $B$, let
\begin{equation}
\theta_{\pipeline}=\operatorname{Train}\!\left(f,\pipeline_c(\rawdata),B\right)
\end{equation}
denote the policy trained after applying pipeline $\pipeline$.
Let $\Pi$ be the set of candidate pipelines and $\tau$ the evaluation distribution.
Let $\utility(\theta)$ denote the reported task, generalization, recovery, or safety performance of policy $\theta$ on $\tau$.
Candidate pipelines should first be compared by expected policy performance on the declared target tasks:
\begin{equation}
\pipeline^{*}=\underset{\pipeline\in\Pi}{\arg\max}\;
\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\!\left[\utility(\theta_{\pipeline})\right].
\label{eq:prep-objective}
\end{equation}
Preparation cost and risk remain important evaluation dimensions, but adding them to the objective would obscure the central question of whether the data improve learning.

The fixed budget in Equation~\ref{eq:prep-objective} prevents a common confound.
If filtering removes half of the data while the epoch count stays fixed, the filtered policy receives fewer optimizer updates and each retained example is repeated differently.
The comparison then changes both data preparation and training compute.

\subsection{Validity Is Not the Same as Usefulness}

An error-free record need not improve a policy.
It may repeat behavior already covered by the training set or provide actions that do not transfer to the target robot.
Conversely, a failed episode may contain a valid approach or recovery even though it is unsuitable as a complete expert demonstration.
We therefore distinguish whether a record is internally valid from whether it adds useful supervision in a specific training context.

This dependence can be expressed by the performance change caused by adding one episode.
For a prepared dataset $\mathcal{D}$, let $\theta_{\mathcal{D}}$ denote a policy trained on $\mathcal{D}$ under the same budget convention.
The pipeline output $\pipeline_c(e)$ contains the training units derived from candidate episode $e$ for context $c$.
Its expected value is:
\begin{equation}
V(e\mid \mathcal{D},c)=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\!\left[
\utility(\theta_{\mathcal{D}\cup\pipeline_c(e)})-
\utility(\theta_{\mathcal{D}})
\right].
\label{eq:conditional-value}
\end{equation}
The equation states a counterfactual question: how would policy performance change if the units derived from $e$ were added to the current dataset?
Exact retraining for every candidate is normally infeasible, so selection scores attempt to estimate this quantity more cheaply.

The value depends on the surrounding dataset because an episode can duplicate existing behavior, fill a coverage gap, or conflict with another action convention.
It also depends on the learner.
RoboMimic shows that policy performance on human demonstrations changes substantially with the learning algorithm, observation space, and training choices~\cite{mandlekar2021what_2108_03298}.
Formal analysis identifies a related mechanism: action divergence and diversity in the observed transitions jointly affect imitation-learning error~\cite{belkhale2023data_2306_02437}.
Together, these results argue against a learner-independent ranking of demonstrations.

\subsection{Four Complementary Axes}

The taxonomy separates four questions that are often collapsed into the word ``quality.''
The axes are complementary descriptions of one method rather than four disjoint method families.
Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy-axes} gives the compact definition, and Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy-section-map} shows how the axes organize the paper.

The \emph{operation axis} asks what changes in the data or in their use.
At a high level, operations make raw records usable, turn usable records into training units, expand or validate those units, and update them after model feedback.
This axis provides the section order: Section~\ref{sec:acquisition} covers usable records, Section~\ref{sec:curation} covers unit construction and assignment, Section~\ref{sec:evolution} covers expansion and validation, and Section~\ref{sec:recipes-engines} covers feedback-driven updates.

The \emph{decision-signal axis} asks why an operation is chosen.
A decision can use evidence contained in the record, judgment supplied by a person or pretrained model, or feedback from the learner and its execution.
The last group is target-specific because it depends on the policy, task, or robot being improved.
Such feedback can answer whether a record helps that target, but it requires training or execution and can inherit errors from the current model.

The \emph{utility-evidence axis} asks what the reported result demonstrates.
Changing a data statistic shows that the operation had its intended local effect.
Agreement with a model or simulator provides proxy evidence.
Only controlled policy training or robot execution tests whether the changed data improve embodied behavior.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} uses this axis to separate these claims and to define comparable experiments.

The \emph{lifecycle axis} asks when the decision changes and who chooses the next operation.
A pipeline may be fixed before training, adapt its sampling during training, or use later rollouts to revise collection and preparation.
In the last case, authority may still remain with a human-designed controller, or an automated agent may choose which operation and diagnostic to run.
This distinction supports the progression from static curation to the data engines and preparation agents studied in Sections~\ref{sec:recipes-engines} and~\ref{sec:agenda}.

We record the transformed object, granularity, and intended objective as secondary attributes rather than additional top-level axes.
These fields tell us, for example, whether an operation changes an entire episode or one transition and whether it targets validity or coverage.
Keeping them secondary preserves a readable taxonomy while still exposing under-studied combinations.
