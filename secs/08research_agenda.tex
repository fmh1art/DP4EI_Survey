\section{Open Challenges and Research Agenda}
\label{sec:agenda}

The taxonomy identifies specific gaps rather than calling generically for ``more data'' or ``better generalization.'' For each challenge, we state what should be measured and which result would count as failure.

\subsection{Calibrating Proxies to Policy Utility}

Most preparation pipelines cannot retrain a policy for every candidate record or mixture.
They need an inexpensive score that ranks candidates by the policy improvement they are expected to produce.
In the notation of Section~\ref{sec:problem}, this means estimating $V(e\mid c,\mathcal{D})$ while the learner, budget, or target distribution may change.
Current estimates use smoothness, information, progress, influence, and foundation-model judgments.
Each score measures a different property and can disagree with the result of policy training.

A promising design would combine technical and semantic features with a small set of policy-training or rollout observations chosen for calibration.
The estimator should report uncertainty and separate correctness, coverage, transfer, and risk rather than hide them in one score.
Evaluation should test whether its ranking selects data that improve held-out learners and tasks under a fixed budget.
An oracle with true defect or utility labels can provide an upper bound.
The estimator is useful when it approaches that bound with substantially fewer training runs or robot queries and fails gradually rather than abruptly under target shift.

\subsection{Estimating Interaction and Marginal Value Below the Episode}

Individual scores miss complementarity.
A grasp segment may be useless without its approach.
Two sources can conflict despite each being useful alone, and a rare failure becomes valuable only when paired with a recovery.
Exact marginal attribution through all data subsets or retraining after removing each unit is infeasible at VLA scale.
Pointwise influence approximations can also ignore sequence dependence and interactions among examples.

Research should model value over hierarchical groups and interactions, from transition $\rightarrow$ primitive $\rightarrow$ episode $\rightarrow$ task $\rightarrow$ source.
Promising methods can approximate group interactions from a limited set of subset-training runs or summaries of training gradients.
A benchmark can include known complementary and conflicting groups and evaluate whether selection preserves the combination under equal compute.
This directly tests whether an algorithm has learned data structure rather than a visible quality artifact.

\subsection{Learnable Cross-Embodiment Action Semantics}

The field lacks a standard that expresses what an action \emph{does} across morphologies and controllers.
Fixed action vectors and masks provide a common storage format but do not align consequences.
A stronger representation would condition on a graph of robot links and joints, the end-effector and controller specification, reference frame, latency, and uncertainty.
Rather than sharing action-vector shape, it should represent the object-centric state change that an action is expected to cause.

The representation should be tested in both directions and by physical outcome.
Mapping a source action to a target embodiment and back should recover the source meaning.
Executing or simulating the target action should produce the same object-centric state change within tolerance.
Held-out morphology and controller families are essential.
A mapping that improves cross-robot policy loss but violates target limits or contact should fail the benchmark.
Data formats should preserve original actions and reversible transformations so a learned standard can replace an earlier one without collecting the data again.

\subsection{Verification of Invisible Physical Errors}

Generative models are usually optimized to produce outputs that look likely or preferred.
They can still create errors that are difficult to see in pixels, such as inconsistent depth across views, an unstable grasp, an unreachable wrist pose, or an action label that does not cause the shown transition.
The research problem is to estimate whether a generated unit is valid for its stated training role.

Progress requires challenge sets with paired plausible and invalid examples at each validation layer, including subtle contact and controller errors.
Verifiers should be evaluated on new generators and assets, not only on outputs from the generator used for calibration.
Any shared training data or architecture between the generator and verifier must be reported.
Uncertain samples should be sent to simulation or real replay rather than accepted automatically.
A verification method should report how many samples pass the checks required for their training role, whether the accepted samples improve downstream training, and what the checks cost.
This replaces an undefined synthetic-data quality score with a sequence of testable decisions.

\subsection{A Schema for Failure, Recovery, and Safety}

Current datasets use inconsistent episode-level success fields and often omit takeover, retry, near miss, or failure cause.
A shared schema should record the outcome definition and its confidence, the first deviation or failure interval, and any violated precondition.
It should link human or automatic interventions to retries, valid prefixes, recovery attempts, and their results.
Safety severity and whether each action remains a valid target must also be explicit.
The schema must allow multiple causes and uncertain temporal boundaries.

The schema's value can be evaluated by holding raw trajectories fixed and testing whether structured routing improves recovery and worst-case safety over success-only, all-data, and binary failure baselines.
Agreement among human annotators and labeling models, together with cross-dataset mapping, is necessary but not sufficient.
The decisive outcome is whether a policy learns to identify and exit failure regions without imitating unsafe actions.

\subsection{Stable, Safe Data Self-Evolution}

A self-evolving engine observes the current policy, changes the training data, and then observes a later policy.
This feedback is delayed because policy training takes time, biased because the current model determines which states are visited, and costly because evidence may require simulation or robot execution.
The update must respond to failures without making the data mixture unstable.
The engine also needs a fixed reference set that protects earlier capabilities and an audit that detects changes in verifier behavior.
It must choose when to collect, generate, relabel, or stop while keeping information gathering safe.

Evaluation should run multi-round closed-loop benchmarks, not one post-training iteration.
Utility should be measured after each round and reported together with cumulative robot cost.
Evaluation should also record worst-group retention, changes in whether predicted confidence matches observed outcomes, error amplification, safety events, and rollback frequency.
Stress tests should seed a biased verifier or generator and measure whether the loop detects and corrects the bias.
A method that obtains a short-term average gain while collapsing rare capabilities is unstable, even if its final aggregate success is high.
The recursive-data collapse result is not a robotics theorem, but it supplies an actionable stress test.
The test should progressively increase the fraction of self-generated experience while measuring coverage of rare cases.
It should compare retaining earlier real data with replacing that data, using the same fixed real reference set in both cases~\cite{shumailov2024collapse}.
RECAP's checkpoint restart, SOP's mixture bounds, and LWD's offline reference provide concrete stability controls that can be tested by removing each one in turn ~\cite{intelligence20250_2511_14759,pan2026sop_2601_03044,wang2026learning_2605_00416}.

\subsection{From Fixed Data Engines to Autonomous Preparation Agents}

Most current engines automate a controller designed by people.
Which buffer to sample, which failures trigger intervention, which generator to call, and when to retrain are fixed in advance.
The L4 problem in Figure~\ref{fig:autonomy-ladder} is harder because an agent must select preparation operators and information-gathering experiments when utility is observed only after a delay and with noise.
For example, after detecting weak performance on deformable objects, it must choose among collecting demonstrations, preserving failed prefixes, requesting corrections, generating scenes, changing a mixture, calibrating a verifier, or first running a diagnostic rollout.
These actions differ in cost, delay, safety, and how clearly the eventual outcome can be attributed to the choice.

This can be studied without granting unrestricted real-robot autonomy.
A benchmark should expose a versioned raw pool, an operator library with explicit input and output schemas, fixed learners, a simulator, limited hidden real-robot audits, a cost model, and deliberately constructed defects or coverage gaps.
At each round an agent receives only the evidence it requested, emits an executable preparation plan and predicted gain, and may promote, abstain, stop, or roll back.
Comparisons should include a fixed expert pipeline, random allocation, adaptive allocation that learns from earlier rounds, an oracle with defect labels, and an LLM planner without learner or rollout feedback.
Metrics should include the policy-performance gap from the best affordable sequence, evidence and robot cost, safety violations, retention of the weakest group, calibration of predicted gains, unnecessary rollbacks, and exact replayability from lineage.

Progress toward such an agent can be tested in stages.
Its transformations must first be valid and reversible.
It must then isolate a data decision with matched controls instead of changing the learner at the same time.
A stronger test asks whether the selected pipeline also helps a held-out learner or deployment shift.
Throughout these stages, the agent must respect authority boundaries, fixed reference data, and safety budgets even when a proxy predicts a gain.
An LLM that writes a fluent pipeline but cannot meet these criteria is an interface to preparation, not an autonomous preparation agent.

\subsection{Cost and Resource Accounting}

Preparation papers rarely include full costs, making an expensive selector appear data-efficient.
Standardized accounts should include human review, model inference, subset-training runs, simulation, storage and decoding, network transfer, target-robot hours, interventions, energy use, and discarded collection.
Benefits should be expressed as utility reached at a total cost and should state how preparation cost is shared across downstream models.

Such accounting makes staged validation comparable.
A deterministic check may remove known corruption, a small model may route obvious semantic cases, and influence or rollout evidence may be reserved for uncertain high-value groups.
The best sequence depends on the deployment context.
Benchmark tracks should use several explicit resource profiles rather than one hidden budget.

\subsection{Bias, Consent, Licensing, and Auditability}

Human and community data introduce privacy, consent, worker, and geographic representation questions.
Web video introduces licensing and identity risk.
Foundation-model filters can systematically reject unfamiliar homes, tools, or bodies, while autonomous collection can record bystanders or unsafe attempts.
These are preparation decisions because filters, retention policies, provenance, and access control determine what enters training.

Dataset documentation should expose source license and consent, sensitive fields, lawful summary variables for demographic or environmental representation, filter rejection rates by subgroup, and known blind spots.
Provenance must survive synthesis.
A generated derivative should retain source and model lineage.
Utility optimization should include worst-group and safety constraints so rare environments are not removed merely because they are harder for a pretrained judge.

\subsection{A Practical Evaluation Path}

The preceding challenges can be studied without first building a general autonomous system.
A useful initial experiment would start from an unchanged raw pool with complete lineage and an open VLA learner.
The available decisions could be restricted to segmentation, a small set of curation signals, source mixture weights, and one validation rule for synthetic data.
Contamination, target shift, and coverage gaps would be varied independently so that each gain can be attributed to a specific decision.

At each round, a controller would choose one preparation decision and purchase a fixed amount of training, simulation, or robot evidence.
Hand-designed and random pipelines would provide reference baselines, while an oracle with hidden defect labels would bound the available improvement.
A data and model version would be promoted only after a matched comparison shows higher policy performance without violating safety or retained-capability constraints.
Evaluation would report the policy-performance gap from the oracle, total resource cost, retention of the weakest group, and whether the full sequence can be replayed from its lineage.

The longer-term hypothesis is that preparation decisions can be learned while remaining auditable, which is the transition from an L3 engine to an L4 agent.
Evidence would reject this hypothesis if learned pipelines fail to transfer beyond their calibration learner, consume more resources than fixed baselines, cannot attribute gains to a data decision, or amplify errors across rounds.
Automation alone is not an academic contribution.
It must improve the correctness--coverage--cost trade-off under controlled evidence and decline authority when the required validation is unavailable.
