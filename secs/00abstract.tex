Embodied foundation models draw on robot demonstrations, human video,
simulation, generated trajectories, and deployment experience.  These records
differ in embodiment, control semantics, timing, annotation, physical validity,
and relevance to the learner.  Data volume alone does not describe how useful
such a mixture will be for training.  This survey examines data preparation for
embodied intelligence, defined as the decisions that turn heterogeneous
interactions into a trainable distribution whose value can be tested downstream.
We review 211 papers and system reports and organize them by the operation
performed, the signal used to make the decision, the evidence used to assess
utility, and the point in the data lifecycle at which the decision occurs.  The
review covers source design, technical validation, cross-embodiment alignment,
semantic enrichment, curation and mixture design, augmentation, synthesis,
verification, and failure-driven updates.  We also compare publicly disclosed
recipes from Qwen-RobotManip and Qwen-RobotWorld, the $\pi$ family, GR00T, and
Gemini Robotics.  Their results are analyzed structurally because their data
units, models, and evaluation protocols are not directly comparable.  Across the
literature, data quality is conditional on the learner and training stage,
useful preparation increasingly operates below the episode level, and synthetic
or self-generated data requires independent validation.  Failed experience is
useful only when its outcome and temporal structure determine how it enters
training.  These findings motivate evaluation protocols that connect intrinsic
diagnostics and proxy scores to controlled policy training and deployment.  They
also clarify the progression from static curation to selection guided by the
current learner, failure-driven post-training, and closed-loop data systems that
repeatedly revise and audit the training distribution.
