Embodied foundation models are trained on a rapidly expanding mixture of robot
demonstrations, human videos, simulation, generated trajectories, and deployment
experience.  These records are not interchangeable: they differ in embodiment,
control semantics, timing, annotation, physical validity, and relevance to the
policy being trained.  Consequently, raw scale is an inadequate account of the
data bottleneck.  This survey studies \emph{data preparation for embodied
intelligence}: the decisions that transform heterogeneous interactions into a
trainable distribution with verifiable downstream value.  We synthesize an
auditable evidence map of 204 papers and system reports through four orthogonal
lenses: the operation performed on data, the signal that guides it, the evidence
used to establish utility, and its position in the data--model lifecycle.  This
view unifies source design, technical validation, cross-embodiment alignment,
segmentation and relabeling, filtering and mixture optimization, augmentation and
synthesis, layered verification, and failure-driven iteration.  Across these
areas, the literature supports five conclusions.  Data quality is conditional on
the learner, task, embodiment, stage, and budget; useful decisions increasingly
occur below the episode level; shape-compatible actions need not be
semantically compatible; synthetic-data generation is limited by validation
rather than visual plausibility alone; and offline pipelines are evolving into
feedback controllers.  We distinguish intrinsic, proxy, and downstream evidence,
explain why results reported under different training budgets cannot be ranked
directly, and derive a research agenda for utility-calibrated metrics,
cross-embodiment semantics, stable self-evolution, and cost-aware evaluation.
