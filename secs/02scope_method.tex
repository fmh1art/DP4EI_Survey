\section{Scope and Review Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

We conduct a systematic \emph{scoping} review rather than a statistical
meta-analysis.  The field lacks a shared intervention, learner, dataset, and
outcome protocol; aggregating success-rate differences across papers would
therefore produce a precise-looking but invalid estimate.  Our goal is instead to
map the design space, code comparable method properties, evaluate the strength of
each type of evidence, and derive hypotheses that can be tested under future
common protocols.

\subsection{Research Questions and Eligibility}

The review is governed by four research questions: (RQ1) Which decisions turn raw
embodied records into training examples or sampling distributions? (RQ2) Which
signals guide those decisions, at what granularity, and with what assumptions?
(RQ3) How is data value validated, and when are reported outcomes comparable?
(RQ4) How does training or deployment experience alter the next data iteration?

A work is eligible when it describes at least one identifiable operation on
embodied data, source design that changes downstream compatibility or coverage,
or an empirical analysis of data composition.  Eligible evidence includes
peer-reviewed conference/journal papers, arXiv papers, dataset and benchmark
papers, and technical reports with sufficient methodological detail.  Official
project or repository documentation is used only for formats, released artifacts,
or implementation facts.  A work is excluded from the core synthesis when it
only consumes an existing dataset, changes only the policy architecture, treats a
generic simulator without a data transformation, concerns navigation/driving
without a transferable preparation mechanism, or lacks enough primary material
to determine its operation and evidence.

\subsection{Discovery and Screening}

The search covers January 2018 through August 1, 2026.  We combine: (i) DBLP and
arXiv queries over conjunctions of robot/embodied/VLA with \emph{data curation},
\emph{demonstration selection}, \emph{quality}, \emph{filtering}, \emph{mixture},
\emph{relabeling}, \emph{segmentation}, \emph{augmentation}, \emph{synthetic
demonstration}, \emph{data engine}, \emph{self-improvement}, and
\emph{cross-embodiment}; (ii) backward and forward citation tracing from central
papers; and (iii) checks of major datasets, benchmarks, labs, industrial model
reports, project pages, and public data documentation.  The exact strings, raw
database responses, query-level errors, and a rerunnable script are included in
the artifact (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).
The resolved snapshot contains 1,160 title-deduplicated database records from
28 queries.  Eight queries that failed transiently in the initial run were
retried successfully; both the original failures and resolved summaries are
retained.  This count is a discovery universe rather than an inclusion count.

Title/abstract screening removes clearly out-of-scope uses of ``data curation''
and ``data engine.''  Full-text screening then requires a recoverable operation,
signal, or data-composition finding.  Duplicate arXiv and published versions are
merged as one work; we cite the published version when its metadata and content
are verifiable, otherwise the arXiv version.  Updated technical reports replace
earlier versions but version-dependent scale claims are retained with their date.
Citation chasing is stopped after a pass produces no new eligible mechanism or
major system.  Newly discovered papers after the cutoff are logged for the next
update rather than silently added.

The resulting evidence map contains 204 unique works: 56 deep reads (method/data
sections, relevant experiments, and limitations checked), 114 method-level reads
(abstract plus directly relevant primary-text sections), and 34 abstract-index
records.  The 170 deep or method-level works form the technical synthesis.  The
34 abstract-index records contribute only to horizon scanning and coverage
diagnostics; no technical claim in this paper relies on them.  This distinction
prevents a large bibliography from masquerading as equally deep evidence.

\subsection{Structured Coding and Evidence Strength}

Each core work is coded by: problem; source and modality; operation; transformed
object and granularity; decision signal; optimization objective; human
involvement; scale and cost; intrinsic and downstream evaluation; generalization
across tasks, environments, learners, and embodiments; assumptions and failure
modes; openness; provenance; publication type; and reading depth.  For composite
systems, we code each material operation separately.  A method that labels,
filters, and then synthesizes is therefore multi-label rather than forced into an
``other'' category.  Terminology is normalized only after recording the authors'
original name: for example, ``selection,'' ``pruning,'' and ``filtering'' are
mapped to a common operation without assuming identical objectives.

We use an evidence ladder rather than treating sources as interchangeable:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Intrinsic evidence} measures properties of the records, such as
    missingness, duplication, smoothness, or labeled noise.
    \item \textbf{Proxy evidence} uses representation distance, information,
    foundation-model scores, physical checks, influence approximations, or
    predicted executability.
    \item \textbf{Downstream evidence} retrains a policy under a controlled budget
    and measures simulation or real execution.  Cross-task, cross-learner, and
    cross-embodiment replication strengthens this level.
\end{enumerate}

Peer-reviewed experiments and reproducible primary papers are treated as stronger
support than unreviewed preprints for the same claim.  Technical reports are
valuable for engineering recipes but their internally selected tasks, models, and
budgets limit causal conclusions.  Project pages establish release facts, not
performance.  We explicitly label an inference when it combines evidence across
papers rather than reproducing an author's conclusion.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

Three limitations remain.  First, industrial preparation pipelines are often
under-documented; public evidence over-represents methods that can be isolated in
papers.  Second, terminology is unstable: relevant work can be framed as data
engines, post-training, imitation learning, world models, or self-improvement.
Multi-query search and snowballing reduce but cannot eliminate this risk.  Third,
the newest 2026 works are predominantly preprints and cannot yet provide the same
evidence strength as mature peer-reviewed results.  We therefore avoid a ranking,
report negative or non-monotonic outcomes when available, and publish the corpus
manifest so omissions and coding decisions can be audited.
