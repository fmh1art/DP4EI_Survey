\section{Expanding and Validating Data: Augmentation and Synthesis}
\label{sec:evolution}

This section covers the third part of the operation axis: expanding prepared units and validating the resulting supervision.
Filtering reallocates a finite pool.
Augmentation and synthesis attempt to create missing coverage.
In embodied learning, a generated image is useful only when it remains coupled to an executable state transition and a valid task.
We organize generation by what is transformed and how its validity can be tested, not by the visual generator family.
A world model here means a model that predicts how an observed scene may evolve.
Figure~\ref{fig:generation-hierarchy} gives this organization and links each intervention family to the new validity condition it creates.

\subsection{A Hierarchy of Generative Interventions}

Observation-level augmentation changes appearance or viewpoint while intending to preserve state and action.
It is inexpensive and often useful for visual robustness, but must respect occlusion and camera-frame actions.
Generating a plausible new view without updating its camera pose or visibility metadata creates a mislabeled action observation.
GenAug and ROSIE use pretrained image generators to alter demonstration scenes while preserving the intended behavior ~\cite{chen2023genaug_2302_06671,yu2023scaling_2302_11550}.
RoVi-Aug extends the intervention to robot-video augmentation, whereas RoboEngine combines generative editing with explicit robot-data engineering ~\cite{chen2024rovi_2409_03403,yuan2025roboengine_2503_18738}.
Together they show the scale advantage of image-space editing.
They also expose its central audit question, namely whether pixels outside the edited region, geometry, action labels, and task success still describe the same transition.

Trajectory-level transformation changes the scene or motion and recomputes actions through geometry, replay, or optimization.
It preserves more action structure than video editing but depends on accurate object frames and on the task remaining valid after transformation.
Mirroring, retiming, and spatial warping are valid only when every coupled observation, action, and task field is transformed consistently, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:acquisition}.

Segment recomposition decomposes demonstrations into object-relative skills and instantiates or stitches them in new scenes.
MimicGen is a representative example: it extracts task segments from a small number of human demonstrations, transforms them with respect to new object configurations, and executes the resulting sequence to obtain additional demonstrations ~\cite{mandlekar2023mimicgen_2310_17596}.
This substantially reduces manual demonstration demand in the paper's task suite, but assumes that task segmentation, object pose, and segment-level invariance are reliable.
Error at a stitch can be physically small yet leave the robot in a state from which the next primitive cannot succeed.

Simulation-based task generation creates assets, scenes, goals, rewards, and expert trajectories.
Its strength is exact machine-readable state and scalable failure generation.
Its weaknesses are generator bias, solver artifacts, and mismatch between simulation and reality.
Variation should be measured in task factors that change behavior and contact, not only in visual texture.
GenSim composes task-generation and planning components, while RoboGen uses generative models to propose tasks, environments, and supervision ~\cite{wang2023gensim_2310_01361,wang2023robogen_2311_01455}.
Such systems can expand nominal task count quickly, but generated tasks inherit the proposer, asset library, reward generator, and planner's combined range of possibilities.
Task count alone is not evidence of behavioral coverage.

Video and world-model generation predicts demonstrations or alternative futures conditioned on language, actions, images, or geometry.
Xiaomi-Robotics-U0 targets embodied synthesis with multiple views and geometric constraints ~\cite{li2026xiaomi_2607_11643}.
Such models can propose rare scenes and future states at much lower marginal cost than real collection, but a photorealistic rollout can hide inconsistent three-dimensional geometry, unreachable motion, or incorrect contact.
Generated pixels do not automatically supply calibrated robot actions.
Recovering actions from pixels is a separate estimation problem whose uncertainty must be stored.

\subsection{The Bottleneck Is Layered Validation}

Generation quality is sometimes evaluated with visual fidelity or a vision--language score.
These tests can reject some defects but cannot establish that a trajectory is executable or useful for training.
The validation stack in Table~\ref{tab:validation-stack} proceeds from cheap technical checks to expensive deployment evidence.
A sample should advance only as far as its intended training role requires: an observation-only pretraining clip may not need action validity, whereas a contact-rich behavior-cloning target must pass kinematic and physical checks.

The layers fail in different ways.
A semantic verifier can accept a visually correct but physically impossible trajectory.
Inverse kinematics can find a joint path that violates force or collision constraints.
A simulator can replay a trajectory under inaccurate contact parameters, and a policy can exploit a simulator artifact.
Agreement between generator and verifier is also weak when they share training data or architecture.
High-risk samples should be checked by models from different families, geometric and physical constraints, and real measurements where possible.

Validation should emit calibrated uncertainty and reason codes.
Hard rejection throws away informative near misses that can train a verifier, world model, or recovery policy.
Routing should reflect the type of evidence available.
Valid action supervision can enter policy training.
Semantically valid video with uncertain actions is better suited to representation learning, while physically invalid samples can serve as negative examples for a verifier.
Ambiguous high-value cases should go to simulation or human review.
Evaluation should report how many samples pass the checks required for their training role per unit cost, not only raw generation throughput.

\subsection{Generative Diversity Can Be Illusory}

Counting prompts or rendered scenes overstates coverage when samples inherit the same generator biases.
Diversity should be measured separately in visual context, physical interaction, behavior strategy, and embodiment.
Near-duplicate video may still contain distinct contact dynamics, while visually diverse generations may induce the same action sequence.
Joint state--action or transition-space diagnostics are more relevant to control than image embeddings alone, an intuition also reflected in SCIZOR's joint-space deduplication ~\cite{zhang2025scizor_2505_22626}.

The value of generated data changes as the policy learns.
A synthetic state that was useful in an early round may become redundant after the policy masters it, while deployment can reveal new failures that an earlier verifier never observed.
Periodic controlled training and rollout audits are therefore needed to update generation priorities.
Generation should target identified coverage gaps and difficult but valid cases.
Separately calibrated verifiers should search for semantic and physical violations rather than reward outputs that merely resemble the generator's training data.
